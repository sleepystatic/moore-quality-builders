Complete Guide to Home Remodeling Permits in San Diego County (2025)

June 2025 11 min read By Moore Quality Builders

One of the most common questions homeowners ask before a remodel is: "Do I actually need a permit for this?" It's a fair question—permits take time, cost money, and feel like red tape when you just want the project done. But skipping a required permit is one of the most expensive mistakes a homeowner can make.

This guide explains exactly which projects require permits in San Diego County, how the application process works, what to expect during inspections, and what happens when unpermitted work is discovered. Whether you're planning a kitchen remodel, bathroom renovation, ADU, or room addition, this is what you need to know before breaking ground.

What Is a Building Permit?

A building permit is official approval from your local government that a proposed construction project meets current building codes, zoning laws, and safety standards. When work is permitted, a building inspector reviews the plans before construction and inspects the work at key stages to verify it was done correctly.

Permits exist to protect homeowners, future buyers, and occupants. Building codes aren't arbitrary—they reflect decades of engineering standards and hard lessons from fires, structural failures, and water damage events. When a contractor pulls a permit, they're putting their license on the line and agreeing to have the work independently verified.

Key Agencies in San Diego County:

  • City of San Diego Development Services: sandiego.gov/development-services
  • San Diego County Planning & Development Services: For unincorporated county areas
  • Individual city departments: Chula Vista, El Cajon, Carlsbad, Oceanside, Escondido, and other cities each have their own permit offices

If you're unsure which jurisdiction applies to your property, look at your property tax bill—the agency listed there is typically your permit authority.

What Requires a Building Permit in San Diego?

As a general rule, any work that affects the structural integrity, electrical system, plumbing system, or fire safety of your home requires a permit. Here's a breakdown by project type:

Kitchen Remodels

  • Moving or adding electrical circuits or outlets
  • Relocating plumbing lines (sink, dishwasher)
  • Installing a new range hood that vents through the exterior wall
  • Removing or modifying a wall (load-bearing or not)
  • Adding a gas line for a range

Does not require a permit: Replacing cabinets in place, new countertops, new flooring, appliance swaps (like-for-like), painting.

Bathroom Remodels

  • Relocating plumbing (toilet, shower, sink)
  • Adding or moving electrical circuits, GFCI outlets
  • Installing a new exhaust fan requiring exterior venting
  • Any structural changes
  • Converting a tub/shower combo to a walk-in shower (in most jurisdictions)

Does not require a permit: Replacing tile over existing substrate, swapping vanity or toilet in same location, new fixtures, painting.

Room Additions & ADUs

  • All new construction adding livable square footage
  • Garage conversions to living space
  • Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs) — always required
  • Enclosed patios or sunrooms
  • Second-story additions

Decks, Patios & Exterior Work

  • Decks over 30 inches above grade
  • Decks attached to the house (regardless of height)
  • New fences over 6 feet tall
  • Retaining walls over 4 feet
  • Detached structures over 120 sq ft (varies by city)

Electrical Work

  • Adding new circuits or subpanels
  • Installing EV charging outlets (240V)
  • Upgrading an electrical panel
  • Any new wiring inside walls

HVAC & Mechanical

  • Installing a new HVAC system
  • Adding ductwork or extending an existing system
  • Installing a new water heater (in most jurisdictions)
  • Adding a fireplace or wood stove

What Does NOT Require a Permit

Not every home improvement project needs official approval. These typically do not require a permit:

  • Painting (interior or exterior)
  • Flooring replacement (carpet, hardwood, LVP, tile over existing substrate)
  • Cabinet replacements in the same location
  • Countertop replacements
  • Appliance replacements (same type, same location)
  • Fixture swaps (faucets, toilets, light fixtures on existing circuits)
  • Replacing windows and doors in existing openings (in most cases)
  • Fences under 6 feet
  • Detached structures under 120 sq ft (sheds, playhouses) — varies by city
💡 When in Doubt, Ask: Your local building department will tell you over the phone whether a permit is required for your specific project. It's a free call and takes five minutes. It's far better than finding out mid-escrow that work needed a permit.

How to Apply for a Building Permit in San Diego

The permit application process varies slightly by city, but the general steps are consistent throughout San Diego County:

Step 1 — Determine Jurisdiction: Identify whether your property falls under city, county, or a special district's authority.
Step 2 — Prepare Your Documents: Gather site plans, floor plans, elevations, structural calculations (if required), and Title 24 energy compliance documents. For larger projects, these are typically prepared by a licensed architect or designer.
Step 3 — Submit Your Application: Most jurisdictions now accept online submissions through their permitting portal. The City of San Diego uses the Development Services online permit system. You can also apply in person.
Step 4 — Plan Review: A building official reviews your plans for code compliance. Simple projects may be approved over the counter; complex projects go through a full plan review cycle (typically 3–10 weeks).
Step 5 — Pay Fees & Receive Permit: Once approved, you pay permit fees and receive your permit. The permit (or permit card) must be posted visibly at the job site during construction.
Step 6 — Schedule Inspections: As work progresses, schedule required inspections. The inspector verifies each stage before work can be covered up (e.g., framing before drywall, rough plumbing before walls are closed).
Step 7 — Final Inspection & Sign-Off: After all work is complete, a final inspection is scheduled. When approved, the permit is closed out and your project is legally complete.

Online Permit Portals (San Diego County):

  • City of San Diego: Development Services — online permitting through the City's website
  • County of San Diego (unincorporated areas): PDS online permit portal
  • Other cities: Each city (Chula Vista, Carlsbad, Oceanside, El Cajon, Escondido, etc.) has its own portal — search "[city name] building permit application"

Permit Costs & Fees

Permit fees in San Diego County are calculated based on the estimated value of the construction work. Here are typical ranges:

Typical Permit Fee Ranges (2025):

  • Minor remodel (cosmetic + one trade): $200–$800
  • Kitchen or bathroom remodel: $500–$2,500
  • Room addition (under 500 sq ft): $1,500–$4,000
  • ADU (full construction): $3,000–$8,000+
  • New home construction: $10,000–$30,000+

Fees vary significantly by city. The City of San Diego calculates fees based on valuation tables; other cities may use flat rates or different multipliers. Your contractor or the permit office can give you an accurate estimate for your specific project.

Additional Fee Categories to Know:

  • Plan check fee: Charged at submission for the cost of reviewing plans (sometimes separate from the permit fee)
  • School fees: Charged on new square footage for additions and ADUs — can add $3–$5 per square foot
  • Impact fees: Charged for larger projects that impact infrastructure; common for ADUs and additions
  • Re-inspection fees: If an inspection fails and requires a return visit, expect $150–$400 per re-inspection

The Inspection Process

Inspections are how the city verifies the work was done correctly. Different project types trigger different inspection requirements:

Common Inspection Types:

Rough Inspections (before walls are closed)

  • Rough framing: Verifies structural framing meets plans before drywall
  • Rough electrical: Checks wiring, panel work, and conduit before walls are closed
  • Rough plumbing: Verifies pipe sizing, slopes, and connections before concealment
  • Rough mechanical: Ductwork and HVAC rough-in before insulation and drywall

Mid-Stage Inspections

  • Insulation inspection: Verifies insulation type and coverage before drywall
  • Waterproofing inspection: Required for shower pans and wet areas in some jurisdictions
  • Foundation inspection: For additions and new construction before concrete pour

Final Inspection

The final inspection verifies the completed project matches the approved plans, all systems are functional, required fixtures are installed, and the space is safe for occupancy. A passed final inspection closes the permit and gives you a Certificate of Occupancy (C of O) for additions and ADUs.

💡 Inspection Tip: Schedule inspections as early as possible—some jurisdictions book 1–2 weeks out. Your contractor should manage inspection scheduling as part of the project timeline. Never cover up rough work before the inspection is passed and signed off.

Permit Timelines by Project Type

How long you'll wait for permit approval depends heavily on the complexity of the project and current backlog at the permitting office:

Over-the-Counter / Express Permits (Same Day to 1 Week)

  • Simple electrical work (adding an outlet, GFCI updates)
  • Water heater replacement
  • Simple plumbing replacements
  • Minor structural alterations with standard plans

Standard Plan Review (2–6 Weeks)

  • Kitchen and bathroom remodels with plumbing/electrical relocation
  • Attached decks
  • Small room additions under 200 sq ft
  • Garage conversions

Full Plan Review (4–12 Weeks)

  • Room additions over 200 sq ft
  • ADU construction
  • Second-story additions
  • Any project requiring structural engineering review

Note: California law (SB 9, AB 68) requires local agencies to approve or deny ADU permit applications within 60 days. For standard permits, timelines are not state-mandated and vary widely by city workload. The City of San Diego regularly updates estimated review times on its website.

💡 Speed Up Your Approval: Complete, well-prepared plans with all required documents are the single biggest factor in fast permit approvals. Incomplete applications are returned for correction and restart the clock. This is one reason working with an experienced contractor pays off—they know exactly what the city wants to see.

Consequences of Unpermitted Work

This is the section most homeowners wish they had read before hiring an unlicensed contractor or taking a DIY shortcut. The consequences of unpermitted work are real, expensive, and often show up at the worst possible time.

When You Try to Sell Your Home

During escrow, buyers typically hire a home inspector and order a permit history report. Unpermitted additions, remodeled rooms, or converted spaces will be flagged. This can:

  • Kill the sale entirely if the buyer's lender won't finance the property
  • Force you to retroactively permit the work (often at higher cost than doing it right originally)
  • Require you to tear out and redo work to bring it to current code
  • Significantly reduce your selling price to compensate the buyer for the risk

Retroactive Permitting ("Permit After the Fact")

San Diego allows retroactive permits, but the process is more expensive and complicated than doing it right the first time. The city may require:

  • Opening walls to expose hidden work for inspection
  • Bringing all work up to current code (not the code at the time it was built)
  • Paying permit fees plus a penalty multiplier (often 2–3x standard fees)
  • Hiring an engineer to certify structural work was done correctly

Insurance Complications

If unpermitted work causes a fire, flood, or structural failure, your homeowner's insurance may deny the claim entirely. The unpermitted work becomes a material misrepresentation of your home's condition.

Fines & Stop-Work Orders

If a building inspector discovers unpermitted work in progress (or following a neighbor complaint), they can issue a stop-work order and levy daily fines until the issue is resolved. Fines vary by city but commonly start at $500–$1,000 per day.

⚠️ Bottom Line: Unpermitted work never truly disappears. It lives in your property records (or conspicuously doesn't), and it becomes your problem—not your contractor's—when it surfaces. The short-term savings of skipping a permit rarely survive contact with the real consequences.

How a Licensed Contractor Handles the Permit Process

One of the most practical benefits of hiring a licensed contractor is that they manage the entire permit process for you. Here's what that looks like in practice:

  • Determines permit requirements: An experienced contractor knows exactly which projects require permits and which don't—no guesswork on your part
  • Prepares or coordinates plans: For complex projects, they work with architects or designers to produce permit-ready drawings
  • Submits the application: Licensed contractors are registered with city permitting systems and can submit directly on your behalf
  • Responds to plan check comments: If the city requests revisions, the contractor handles the back-and-forth
  • Schedules and manages inspections: The contractor coordinates all required inspections so work isn't delayed and nothing gets covered up prematurely
  • Stands behind the work: When a licensed contractor pulls a permit, their license is attached to the project—they're legally accountable for code compliance

What to Ask Your Contractor About Permits:

  • "Will this project require a permit?" — and get it in writing if the answer is no
  • "Who is responsible for pulling the permit?" — it should always be the contractor, not the homeowner
  • "Is the permit fee included in your estimate, or is it extra?"
  • "How do you handle inspections and who schedules them?"
  • "Can I see your California contractor's license number?" — verify at CSLB.ca.gov
⚠️ Red Flag: Any contractor who asks the homeowner to pull the permit in their own name is a major warning sign. In California, the homeowner can legally pull an owner-builder permit—but if something goes wrong, all liability transfers to you. A licensed contractor should always pull permits under their own license.
